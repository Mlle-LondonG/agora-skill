# Skills, practices and the error manual

Evidence labels: **[E+]** solid, replicated · **[E]** moderate or context-dependent · **[P]** practical suggestion. Sources in `evidence.md`.

## 1. Skills matrix
| Skill | Main practice | Metric | Frequent error | Adjustment if it fails | Focus week |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deep attention | Device-free blocks with a defined outcome | Uninterrupted focus minutes; interruptions per session | Studying with notifications on | 15-min blocks; phone out of the room | W1 |
| Long-term memory | Retrieval + spacing (FSRS/Leitner) | Delayed recall % | Rereading instead of retrieving | Less new content, more reviews | W2 |
| First principles | Self-explanation and "why?" chains | Explanation rubric (mechanism) | Memorising formulas without deriving them | Worked examples with self-explanation | W3 |
| Critical reading | Argument map and a contrary source | Essay rubric (evidence) | Summarising instead of evaluating | Thesis–evidence–assumption template | W4 |
| Logical and causal reasoning | Inference sets, counterexamples, causal diagrams | % items correct; valid counterexamples | Confusing correlation with causation | Simpler items with immediate feedback | W5 |
| Probabilistic reasoning and decisions | Base rates, expected value, pre-mortem | Memo quality; calibration | Judging a decision only by its outcome | Write the reasoning before knowing the outcome | W6 |
| Quantitative problems | Graded, interleaved problems: understand, plan, execute, check | Valid solutions per level; time to a valid solution | Looking at the solution before 10 min | Hint ladder; drop a level | W7 |
| Argumentative writing | Weekly essay with an objection | Essay rubric | Vague thesis | Rewrite only thesis and objection until precise | W8 |
| Oral communication and defence | Recorded explanation; supervision | Explanation rubric; follow-up answers | Reciting a memorised script | Unexpected follow-up questions | W8, W12 |
| Combinatorial creativity | ≥15 alternatives; cross-domain analogies | Distinct alternatives; % viable | Settling on the first idea | Separate generating from judging | W9 |
| Transfer | Problems with the same structure and a different surface | Transfer rubric | Always practising the same format | Vary contexts; interleave | W10 |
| Metacognition | Predict before grading; error log | Calibration gap; % repeated errors | Mistaking familiarity for mastery | Per-item predictions; blind self-grading | All |

## 2. Core practices
Each practice states: what to do · why it works · when to use it · how to measure it · common error · example.

1. **Active learning** [E+ in university STEM]. What: produce something in every block (answer, solve, explain) instead of only receiving. Why: in meta-analyses of STEM courses, active learning beats passive lecturing on performance and pass rates. When: always; it is the umbrella for everything else. Measure: share of session time spent on steps 2, 4 and 5 (target ≥60 %). Error: calling verbatim note-taking "active". Example: after 10 minutes of reading, close the book and solve a problem.
2. **Retrieval practice** [E+]. What: answer without looking (blank page, questions, problems) before rereading. Why: retrieving consolidates memory more than restudying and exposes real gaps. When: at the start of every session and the end of every block. Measure: recall % without notes. Error: peeking "just for a second"; recognition questions that are too easy. Example: write the definition of the derivative and 3 rules from memory before opening the chapter.
3. **Spaced repetition** [E+]. What: review at expanding intervals (FSRS or Leitner). Why: distributed practice yields more retention than massed practice; the best gap grows with how long you need to remember. When: daily, in step 2. Measure: % correct on due cards; monthly sample of mature cards. Error: reviewing only what you already know, or letting due cards pile up. Example: a recursion card seen on Monday comes back on Tuesday, Friday and two weeks later.
4. **Deliberate practice** [E: structured practice matters, though it explains only part of performance differences]. What: practise just above the current level, with a specific goal and immediate feedback. Why: informative errors and correction drive improvement; comfortable repetition does not. When: step 4. Measure: valid solutions at the assigned level; trend in time to a valid solution. Error: repeating what already works. Example: 5 L3 related-rates problems with immediate correction, instead of 20 mechanical derivatives.
5. **Interleaving** [E: clearest benefit in maths and category learning; smaller or none in other tasks]. What: mix problem types without announcing which is which. Why: it forces choosing the strategy, not just executing it. When: once each type is mastered separately (from W2–W3). Measure: % correct on mixed sets vs blocked sets. Error: interleaving before understanding each type. Example: a shuffled set of integrals by substitution, by parts and by partial fractions.
6. **Elaboration and self-explanation** [E]. What: ask "why is this true?" and explain every step of a worked example to yourself. Why: it links new information to what you know and exposes inconsistencies. When: step 3, especially with worked examples. Measure: explanation rubric (mechanism). Error: paraphrasing without explaining the cause. Example: "This step divides by x because… and is only valid if x ≠ 0."
7. **Worked examples with fading** [E+ for beginners]. What: study a full solution, then one with steps hidden, then solve alone. Why: beginners overload when solving blind; for experts the effect reverses (expertise reversal). When: Foundations level and new topics. Measure: steps completed without help. Error: staying with full examples once the learner can solve alone. Example: full example → example without the last 2 steps → new problem.
8. **Problem-based learning** [E mixed: improves application skills more than factual knowledge; inefficient for novices without guidance]. What: start from an authentic problem and study what is needed to solve it. Why: it gives purpose and a relevance criterion. When: from Intermediate, or with scaffolding at Foundations. Measure: quality of the setup; transfer rubric. Error: throwing a novice into an open problem without guidance. Example: "How could your home use 20 % less electricity?" to study energy and power.
9. **Project-based learning** [P backed by limited E]. What: a real product (report, program, prototype) that requires integrating what was learned. Why: it forces integration and real-world feedback. When: W11–W12 and topic cycles. Measure: project rubric (`projects.md`). Error: a project so big it never ends. Example: a script that analyses your expenses and produces a monthly report.
10. **Case method** [P: established practice in business, law and public-policy schools; limited experimental evidence]. What: analyse a real situation, define the problem, weigh evidence, choose and defend a decision. Why: it trains judgement under uncertainty. When: the weekly case (day 3). Measure: memo rubric and quality of alternatives considered. Error: looking for "the right answer" instead of defending a reasoned decision. Example: decide whether a café should raise prices after a drought makes coffee more expensive.
11. **Peer instruction** [E in university physics]. What: answer a conceptual question alone, discuss with someone who answered differently, answer again. Why: committing first and justifying afterwards exposes conceptual errors. When: concepts with typical misconceptions. Measure: % correct before and after discussion. Error: discussing before committing to an answer. Example: "At the top of a vertical throw, is the acceleration zero?" (No: it is still g; the velocity is zero.) Without classmates: simulated peer (`tutor.md` §3).
12. **Socratic tutoring or supervision** [E: one-to-one tutoring has moderate to large effects; the specific Cambridge format is an institutional practice, not a tested treatment]. What: hand in your own work and defend it under demanding questions. Why: it surfaces assumptions and gaps. When: day 6 every week and on request. Measure: rubrics and errors found. Error: turning the supervision into a lecture. Example: `tutor.md`.
13. **Writing to think** [E: small effect, larger when writing includes metacognitive prompts]. What: write answers, essays and memos as a tool to clarify reasoning. Why: writing exposes logical leaps that go unnoticed in speech. When: weekly written work and every tutoring synthesis. Measure: essay rubric. Error: writing to fill a word count. Example: 300 words on "what I still don't understand about X and why".
14. **Feedback, revision and iteration** [E+, with nuance: feedback on the task and the process helps; feedback aimed at the person can hurt performance]. What: get concrete critique, fix, produce a version 2. Why: it closes the gap between the current result and the goal. When: steps 6–7 and every piece of work. Measure: rubric gain from v1 to v2; % repeated errors. Error: reading the feedback without rewriting. Example: essay v1 (2.5/4) → supervision → v2 (3.2/4).

**On "learning styles"** (visual, auditory…): there is no evidence that matching teaching to a declared style improves learning. Ágora adapts to the subject and to performance, not to style.

## 3. What Ágora takes from each institution
None of these universities uses a single method; practices vary by faculty, course and teacher. Ágora borrows concrete practices, not "the X method".

| Institution | Recognisable practice | What Ágora takes | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| Harvard | Case method (business, law, government); peer instruction (Mazur, physics); argued discussion | Cases with a defended decision; conceptual question → answer → confrontation → new answer | Weekly case, W6, practice 11 |
| MIT | Learning by doing (motto *mens et manus*), rigorous problem sets, labs, design projects | Most session time spent solving; graded problems with checking | Step 4 every session, W7, STEM project |
| Cambridge | Supervisions: independent work (essay or problem sheet) discussed in very small groups (usually 1–3) with a supervisor | Weekly work defended before the tutor | Day 6, W8, W12 |
| Stanford | Human-centred design (d.school), interdisciplinary projects, prototyping and iteration | Prototype early, test with users, iterate on feedback | W9, design project |

## 4. Error manual
| Error | Why it fails | Fix in Ágora | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passive rereading | Builds fluency and familiarity, not retrieval | Close the material and retrieve; one read before self-questioning | Many reading minutes with recall <80 % |
| Excessive highlighting | Does not force selecting or organising; rated low-utility by the evidence | Turn every highlight into a question | More than a third of the page highlighted; can't say what question it answers |
| Mistaking familiarity for mastery | Recognising is not recalling or applying | Predict before grading; transfer problems | Overconfidence >15 points; "I get it but can't do it" |
| Studying without questions | Without a question there is no relevance criterion | Mandatory guiding question (step 3) | Can't say what problem the studied idea solves |
| Avoiding hard exercises | Without difficulty, learning doesn't last (desirable difficulties) | The assigned-level problem comes first in step 4 | Several sessions with zero L3 and L4 |
| Memorising without understanding | Doesn't transfer and fades fast | Self-explanation; "why" cards | Definitions right, transfer ≤1 |
| Not sleeping or resting | Sleep consolidates memory; fatigue multiplies errors | Sleep rule and 6+1 | Sleep <6 h with falling recall |
| Measuring hours instead of results | Hours are not results | Metrics in `evaluation.md` | High minutes with flat metrics |
| Avoiding critique | Without feedback, errors set in | Weekly supervision; show work to someone | Two weeks without outside feedback |
| Copying solutions too early | Removes the generation effort and creates an illusion of understanding | Minimum attempt (SKILL.md §7) and the hint ladder | `max_hint` ≥H4 often |
| Using AI to replace thinking | In a controlled trial, unrestricted chatbot access improved practice but worsened the exam taken without AI; a tutor-style design mitigated the harm | Own attempt first; AI critiques, questions and verifies, never writes the work | Work you can't explain or rebuild without AI |
